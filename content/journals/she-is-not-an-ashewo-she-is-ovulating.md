# SHE IS NOT AN ASHEWO. SHE IS OVULATING. BUT THAT'S NOT EVEN THE CRAZIEST PART.

*The uncomfortable truth about women's bodies, sexual desire, hormonal changes, menstrual pain and a society that understands so little but judges so much.*

**The Crazy Journal | November 2026**

> Apparently, a woman can be intelligent on Monday, sexy on Wednesday, angry on Friday and in pain on Sunday, and society will find a way to make every single one of those things a character problem. We live in a society that expects women to manage biological experiences we barely understand, while simultaneously judging them for how those experiences affect their lives. Maybe women's hormones were never the biggest problem—maybe it was our confidence in judging a body we hadn't bothered to understand.

![She Is Not An Ashewo. She Is Ovulating.](/images/journals/women-bodies-and-judgment.jpg)

**Apparently, a woman can be intelligent on Monday, sexy on Wednesday, angry on Friday and in pain on Sunday, and society will find a way to make every single one of those things a character problem.**

What an extraordinary world.

I came across a rather hilarious explanation of the menstrual cycle.

The speaker confidently divided a woman's month into four phases.

The bleeding phase.

The peaceful phase.

The *ashewo* phase.

And the phase where apparently everybody should avoid provoking her because her hormones are preparing for war.

I laughed.

Especially at the part where a woman suddenly becomes Albert Einstein during ovulation and can supposedly complete an essay in thirty minutes.

But the more I thought about it, the less funny it became.

Because hidden beneath the comedy is something we don't discuss seriously enough.

**We live in a society that expects women to manage biological experiences we barely understand, while simultaneously judging them for how those experiences affect their lives.**

And when someone finally attempts to explain those experiences, we often replace one collection of stereotypes with another.

So I started asking questions.

Why does female sexual desire attract so much moral judgement?

Why is menstrual pain so easily dismissed?

Why are women sometimes treated as emotionally unreliable simply because they menstruate?

And why do we insist on explaining an entire human being through the movements of two hormones?

Let's talk.

And please, nobody should take this personally.

Actually, perhaps some people should.

### First, who exactly appointed society the moral police of female desire?

Let's begin with that wonderfully Nigerian word.

*Ashewo.*

A word that can instantly transform a discussion about sexuality into a judgement about somebody's character.

A woman expresses sexual interest.

Ashewo.

She feels attractive and confident.

Ashewo.

She openly discusses her sexual needs.

Ashewo.

Yet we live in a society where male sexual desire is often treated as evidence of masculinity, while female sexual desire can become evidence of moral failure.

How convenient.

**Apparently, desire becomes respectable or disgraceful depending on who is experiencing it.**

Now, the original speaker makes an interesting point about ovulation.

Hormonal changes around ovulation can be associated with increased sexual desire in some women.

That's real.

But here is what we must understand: it doesn't happen identically to everybody.

Some women notice increased desire around ovulation. Others experience it at different points in their cycle. Some notice little consistent change.

And wanting sex doesn't automatically mean a person is ovulating.

Human sexuality is influenced by relationships, emotions, physical health, stress, personal preferences and many other factors.

Yet somehow, we want to reduce a woman's sexual expression to a single biological explanation.

Perhaps the more important question isn't why women experience desire.

**It's why we have constructed a culture in which a woman needs a biological explanation to defend having a perfectly human feeling.**

A woman doesn't need to apologise for experiencing sexual desire.

She is still responsible for her decisions, just as a man is.

Desire isn't consent.

Desire isn't an instruction to act.

And desire isn't evidence of moral corruption.

Maybe we should finally learn the difference.

### The menstrual cycle is not a rotating collection of personalities.

Now let's address the idea that a woman becomes four different people every month.

It's entertaining.

But scientifically, it's misleading.

The menstrual cycle involves coordinated changes in reproductive hormones and the ovaries and uterus.

Menstruation involves shedding the uterine lining.

The follicular phase begins on the first day of menstruation and involves follicles developing in the ovaries, alongside changing oestrogen levels.

Ovulation occurs when an ovary releases an egg.

Then the luteal phase follows, with progesterone rising as the body prepares for a possible pregnancy. If pregnancy doesn't occur, hormone levels fall and the next period begins.

That's the basic biology.

The phases are not four completely separate personality compartments. Menstruation overlaps with the follicular phase, and ovulation is an event within the ovarian cycle.

And they certainly don't provide a universally accurate timetable for when every woman will become calm, creative, sexually interested or irritable.

**A woman's personality is not a monthly subscription that automatically renews into four different characters.**

And no, the follicular phase isn't necessarily the only time you're meeting the "real" woman.

What does that even mean?

Is she less real when she's bleeding?

Less real when she's exhausted?

Less real when she's experiencing premenstrual symptoms?

Or do we simply prefer the version of her that is easiest for everybody else to deal with?

That's a more disturbing possibility.

Because if a woman is only considered her authentic self when she's cheerful, calm, energetic and sexually convenient, perhaps we're not actually interested in understanding her.

Perhaps we're interested in selecting the version that requires the least consideration from us.

### Science has some bad news for the people who think women become stupid during their periods.

This is where things become particularly interesting.

A 2025 meta-analysis examined 102 studies involving 3,943 participants and found no robust evidence of systematic changes in objectively measured cognitive performance across the menstrual cycle.

The researchers looked at areas including attention, creativity, intelligence, executive functioning and verbal abilities.

And they did not find compelling evidence that women's thinking abilities reliably collapse during menstruation or dramatically improve around ovulation.

Imagine that.

For years, women have faced jokes and assumptions suggesting that their hormones make them irrational or incapable of sound judgement.

Yet the research does not support a general conclusion that a menstruating woman is intellectually less competent.

**Maybe the most irrational person in the room is the one who thinks another person's reproductive cycle determines their intelligence.**

Of course, physical symptoms can affect concentration, comfort and daily functioning.

Anyone experiencing severe pain, fatigue or poor sleep may find certain tasks more difficult.

But that isn't evidence that women become intellectually defective during particular phases of their cycle.

And it certainly isn't a reasonable basis for excluding women from responsibility or leadership.

We need to be careful about what happens when humour becomes a stereotype, and a stereotype becomes an excuse for discrimination.

### Menstrual pain is not an attitude problem.

This part genuinely bothers me.

A woman says she's experiencing painful cramps.

Someone tells her to stop exaggerating.

She says the pain is affecting her ability to work.

Someone reminds her that menstruation is natural.

She asks for rest.

Someone suggests she's being dramatic.

But since when did something being natural mean it couldn't be painful?

Childbirth is natural.

That doesn't make it painless.

Illness can involve natural biological responses.

That doesn't make suffering imaginary.

Menstrual cramps can be related to chemicals called prostaglandins, which contribute to uterine contractions. Some people experience relatively mild discomfort, while others experience pain severe enough to interfere with school, work and ordinary activities.

Conditions such as endometriosis, adenomyosis and fibroids can also cause significant menstrual pain.

And here's the uncomfortable part.

**Some people have been taught to endure severe pain for so long that they no longer recognise it as something deserving medical attention.**

They assume suffering is simply the price of having a female reproductive system.

No.

Pain that repeatedly disrupts someone's life deserves to be taken seriously and assessed appropriately.

And perhaps we should ask why society often praises women's endurance instead of examining the circumstances that make so much endurance necessary.

You shouldn't need to collapse before someone believes you're in pain.

### And no, PMDD is not just the phase where a woman wants to slap everybody.

This is one of the most important misconceptions in the original explanation.

Premenstrual dysphoric disorder, or PMDD, is not another name for the luteal phase.

It is a serious condition affecting some people who menstruate.

It can involve intense emotional and physical symptoms before menstruation, including severe irritability, anxiety, depression, hopelessness and difficulty functioning.

In some cases, it can involve suicidal thoughts.

That is not comedy.

That is not simply a woman being troublesome.

And it is not something every menstruating woman experiences.

PMDD is associated with sensitivity to hormonal changes, rather than simply meaning someone has unusually high or low hormone levels.

Symptoms generally improve after menstruation begins, and effective treatments and support are available.

**Imagine having a genuine health condition while everyone around you treats your distress as a personality defect.**

How isolating must that be?

But I also want to make another distinction.

Understanding the influence of hormones doesn't mean that every action becomes acceptable.

Being emotionally overwhelmed doesn't give anyone unlimited permission to abuse others.

Just as experiencing anger doesn't justify violence, experiencing hormonal symptoms doesn't eliminate responsibility for how we treat people.

However, responsibility should exist alongside compassion, not replace it.

The sensible response is neither "You're crazy" nor "Your hormones excuse everything."

It is understanding the condition, recognising its effects and finding appropriate support.

### The problem with some men is that they want the woman without understanding the human being.

This is where relationships enter the discussion.

I've noticed how easily conversations about women become conversations about what men expect from them.

She should be affectionate.

But not too interested in sex.

She should be emotionally expressive.

But not emotional enough to become inconvenient.

She should be hardworking.

But never too tired.

She should be understanding.

But never require much understanding herself.

What exactly are we expecting?

A partner?

Or a carefully programmed companion whose biological systems never interrupt our convenience?

And before anybody starts shouting, men have their own emotional and physiological challenges, too.

Those deserve attention.

But we're discussing a particular contradiction here.

**How can you claim to love someone while treating their recurring health experiences as irritating interruptions to your comfort?**

You don't have to understand every aspect of reproductive biology.

You don't have to become a gynaecologist.

But surely you can listen.

Surely you can ask questions.

Surely you can recognise that a woman's experience of her body may be different from what you imagine.

And perhaps most importantly, you can stop using phrases such as "She must be on her period" whenever a woman disagrees with you.

That statement may appear harmless.

But it can become a convenient way to dismiss an argument without addressing its substance.

Maybe she isn't hormonal.

Maybe your behaviour is actually annoying.

Maybe the problem she's complaining about is real.

**Not every disagreement with a woman is a symptom of her menstrual cycle. Sometimes you're simply wrong.**

What a revolutionary possibility.

### But women also need to stop allowing social media to explain their entire identities.

Now let me turn the criticism around.

Because the solution to ignorance isn't another form of ignorance dressed in empowering language.

I've seen how easily online content transforms complex medical information into universal rules.

During this phase, you're a genius.

During that phase, you're irresistible.

During another phase, you're emotionally unstable.

And during the next, you're supposedly your true self.

It's catchy.

It's relatable.

It gets engagement.

But does it accurately describe your individual experience?

Maybe.

Maybe not.

**You cannot discover your entire biological identity from a ninety-second video, no matter how confidently the creator speaks.**

Menstrual cycles vary.

Symptoms vary.

Some people use hormonal contraception that changes their usual hormonal patterns.

Some have irregular cycles.

Others experience significant symptoms that need medical assessment.

And some don't experience the dramatic changes described online at all.

This is why personal observation can be more useful than blindly accepting generalisations.

Track your cycle if it helps.

Notice recurring symptoms.

Pay attention to changes in your mood, energy, pain and wellbeing.

But don't turn every emotional experience into a hormonal diagnosis.

If you're angry because someone disrespected you, perhaps you're simply angry because someone disrespected you.

If you're tired because you've worked for twelve hours, perhaps you're exhausted.

If you're unhappy in a relationship, don't automatically assume the problem is progesterone.

Sometimes biology contributes.

Sometimes circumstances contribute.

Sometimes both do.

**Understanding your hormones should help you understand yourself, not make you suspicious of every feeling you experience.**

### There is something deeply hypocritical about how society treats women's bodies.

We expect women to menstruate quietly.

Experience pain discreetly.

Manage reproductive health privately.

Maintain productivity consistently.

Remain emotionally pleasant.

And somehow never allow any biological reality to become inconvenient to other people.

Meanwhile, we celebrate motherhood.

We romanticise pregnancy.

We praise women's capacity to endure suffering.

We describe women as extraordinarily strong.

But when they ask for understanding, appropriate medical care or reasonable support, the conversation suddenly becomes complicated.

**We celebrate women's strength while sometimes refusing to acknowledge the cost of what they're expected to endure.**

And perhaps this is why menstrual education shouldn't be treated as a subject only girls need to understand.

Boys should learn it.

Partners should understand it.

Parents should discuss it without shame.

Healthcare professionals should take symptoms seriously.

Employers should recognise that health needs are not automatically evidence of professional weakness.

This doesn't mean assuming that every woman is suffering or incapable of working during menstruation.

That would simply replace neglect with paternalism.

It means making room for individual realities rather than forcing everyone into a single stereotype.

Because respect is not about treating women as fragile.

**It's about refusing to demand that they pretend their bodies don't exist.**

### My final thought: perhaps the real problem isn't women's hormones. It's our obsession with judging women.

I've come to an uncomfortable conclusion.

We have an extraordinary ability to turn ordinary human experiences into moral accusations when they involve women.

She expresses desire.

We question her character.

She experiences pain.

We question her strength.

She becomes angry.

We question her emotional stability.

She makes a difficult decision.

We question whether she's being hormonal.

And even when she succeeds, someone may suggest her achievements depend on which phase of her cycle she's in.

What a ridiculous burden.

A woman should not have to prove that her biology is irrelevant before her intelligence is respected.

She shouldn't have to hide her pain to appear competent.

She shouldn't have to apologise for ordinary sexual desire.

And she shouldn't have to pretend that hormonal changes never affect her simply to avoid being stereotyped.

But I also don't believe women's experiences should be reduced entirely to hormones.

**A woman is not simply a uterus with opinions.**

She's a thinking, feeling, deciding human being with a personality, experiences, values, ambitions, relationships and responsibilities.

Her biology matters.

But it doesn't explain everything about her.

And perhaps that's the point we keep missing.

The original speaker was right to challenge the shame surrounding female desire and to encourage women to understand their menstrual cycles.

But the conversation must go deeper than assigning four entertaining personalities to four biological stages.

Because the objective should not be to replace "women are crazy" with "women are four different people every month."

Both explanations are inadequate.

The objective should be understanding.

Actual understanding.

Not the sort that turns into another excuse for judgement.

Not the sort that assumes every woman experiences the same symptoms.

And certainly not the sort that uses biology to dismiss someone's autonomy.

So here's the question I'm leaving in The Crazy Journal:

**IF WE UNDERSTOOD WOMEN'S BODIES BETTER, WOULD WE ACTUALLY TREAT WOMEN BETTER—OR WOULD WE SIMPLY DEVELOP MORE SOPHISTICATED EXCUSES FOR JUDGING THEM?**

Because information doesn't automatically produce empathy.

A man can understand ovulation and still disrespect women.

A woman can understand her cycle and still be ashamed of her sexuality.

A society can teach reproductive biology and still dismiss menstrual pain.

And a workplace can celebrate equality while quietly treating ordinary reproductive health needs as professional inconveniences.

Perhaps the real breakthrough isn't simply knowing what oestrogen and progesterone do.

Perhaps it's learning to recognise the dignity of the human being experiencing those changes.

So no.

She isn't automatically an *ashewo* because she feels sexual desire.

She isn't automatically irrational because she's menstruating.

She isn't automatically at her intellectual peak because she's ovulating.

And she isn't automatically suffering from PMDD because she's irritable before her period.

**She's a human being.**

A complicated one.

Just like you.

Except that society has somehow decided her biology requires a running commentary from everybody who has an opinion.

And that, to me, is the craziest part of the entire discussion.

**Maybe women's hormones were never the biggest problem.**

**Maybe it was our confidence in judging a body we hadn't bothered to understand.**
