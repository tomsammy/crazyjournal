# NVIDIA CRASHED. THE CONSPIRACY DIDN'T. AND NOW THEY WANT YOU TO BUY THE DIP.

*The uncomfortable truth about Jensen Huang, Wall Street, the AI gold rush, market manipulation theories and the dangerous business of selling investors a beautiful story.*

**The Crazy Journal | November 2026**

> A company loses almost $2 trillion in market value. Its legendary CEO resigns amid a scandal. Investors panic. And somewhere on the internet, a man has already figured out exactly what happened—before inviting you to join his Discord trading community. What if the most dangerous part of this story isn't what Wall Street is doing to investors, but what financial content creators are doing to people's understanding of Wall Street?

![Nvidia Crashed. The Conspiracy Didn't. And Now They Want You to Buy the Dip.](/images/journals/nvidia-crash-conspiracy.jpg)

**A company loses almost $2 trillion in market value. Its legendary CEO resigns amid a scandal. Investors panic. And somewhere on the internet, a man has already figured out exactly what happened.**

Apparently, the whole thing might be an elaborate scheme.

Big financial institutions.

Media manipulation.

Artificial panic.

Cheap shares.

And a carefully orchestrated opportunity for the rich to become richer.

Interesting.

Very interesting.

Then, just when you're beginning to believe you've uncovered one of the greatest financial conspiracies in history, the man explaining everything announces that he has invested $170,000 in the stock.

And if you'd like to follow his trades, there's a Discord community waiting for you.

**What a beautiful coincidence.**

I've been examining the recent collapse in Nvidia's share price, and I came across a video attempting to explain what happened.

At first, I was intrigued.

The argument seemed compelling.

A massively profitable company.

A scandal involving its CEO.

An enormous decline in market value.

And suggestions that powerful institutions might be manufacturing fear to accumulate shares at a discount.

But as I examined the claims, something became increasingly uncomfortable.

**What if the most dangerous part of this story isn't what Wall Street is doing to investors, but what financial content creators are doing to people's understanding of Wall Street?**

Because there is a difference between investigating suspicious market behaviour and creating a conspiracy to explain something you don't fully understand.

And that difference can be extraordinarily expensive.

### 1. Let's start with what actually happened.

Nvidia has experienced a substantial decline.

Its shares closed at a record $239.24 on October 6, 2026.

By November 16, they had fallen to $156.14.

That's approximately a 34.7% decline.

Not half.

A significant fall, certainly, but financial accuracy matters.

At its peak, Nvidia's valuation approached $5.8 trillion. By mid-November, approximately $1.8 trillion in market capitalisation had evaporated.

Then came the personal scandal involving Jensen Huang.

Reports emerged about extramarital relationships, including one involving a businesswoman suspected by US authorities of links to Chinese intelligence.

Huang acknowledged infidelity, and Nvidia's board investigated related concerns.

On November 12, Huang resigned as chief executive, with Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress appointed interim CEO.

Now, an important distinction.

**The allegation of Chinese espionage is not the same thing as verified proof that the woman involved was a Chinese intelligence operative.**

China has denied the accusation. Reuters has reported that it could not independently establish whether she worked for the Chinese government.

That uncertainty matters.

Because once somebody removes the word *alleged* from a financial story, the reader may begin treating suspicion as established fact.

And suspicion makes excellent content.

It doesn't always make excellent investment research.

### 2. A CEO's private life can become a very public financial problem.

Let me challenge another assumption in the video.

The narrator suggests that an affair doesn't really affect the company's fundamentals, apart from potentially removing the CEO.

Apart from removing the CEO?

**Sir, that is not a minor detail.**

We're talking about Jensen Huang.

The founder who helped build Nvidia into one of the defining companies of the artificial-intelligence revolution.

The executive whose vision, relationships and strategic decisions became deeply associated with the company's identity.

You cannot spend years telling investors that a particular CEO is an extraordinary competitive advantage and then insist that losing him is financially irrelevant.

Leadership is part of corporate risk.

Corporate governance is part of financial analysis.

Succession planning matters.

And when allegations involve possible intelligence concerns or relationships within the company, the questions extend beyond personal morality.

They become questions about judgement, disclosure, conflicts of interest and whether governance processes functioned properly.

That doesn't automatically justify every dollar of Nvidia's decline.

Nor does it prove the business cannot prosper without Huang.

But pretending that removing a founder-CEO has no possible bearing on valuation is intellectually careless.

**If leadership helped create the premium investors were willing to pay, leadership uncertainty can contribute to the disappearance of that premium.**

The real question is how much of that premium should disappear.

And that requires analysis.

Not gossip.

### 3. The affair theory sounds fascinating. Unfortunately, the historical comparisons don't establish it.

The video presents several supposedly similar events.

Jeff Bezos and Amazon in 2019.

Bill Gates and Microsoft in 2021.

Intel's CEO in 2018.

A Google executive in 2013.

The claim is that personal scandals caused stock prices to fall before they quickly recovered.

And somehow, this recurring pattern suggests an orchestrated financial opportunity.

But there's a problem.

**A chart showing that one event happened before another doesn't prove the first event caused the second.**

Jeff Bezos's personal life became public in January 2019. But Amazon was also moving within broader market conditions, and reporting at the time did not establish that the scandal was responsible for its stock movements.

Bill Gates's relationship controversy resurfaced in May 2021, but Gates was no longer Microsoft's chief executive. He had left that position many years earlier.

In 2018, Intel's Brian Krzanich did resign because of a relationship that violated company policy. But Intel's stock response was mixed, and the company simultaneously issued improved financial guidance.

And in Google's 2013 example, the relationship controversy concerned co-founder Sergey Brin, not the company's then-CEO Larry Page.

Those distinctions are not small details.

They're the difference between research and storytelling.

If you want to demonstrate that corporate sex scandals predict stock-price recoveries, you need more than four conveniently selected charts.

You need a defined sample.

You need to examine companies where the stock didn't recover.

You need to account for broader market movements, earnings announcements, interest rates and sector performance.

You need to establish whether any observed return is statistically unusual.

Otherwise, you're selecting examples that support your preferred explanation.

**You haven't discovered a market pattern. You've discovered how convincing a pattern can look when you ignore everything that contradicts it.**

And that is a dangerous habit for anyone risking real money.

### 4. Then came the claim that three institutions control 90% of America's news stations.

This is where I became particularly sceptical.

The video names Vanguard, BlackRock and State Street.

These are enormous investment-management institutions.

They hold significant stakes across many publicly traded companies, including technology and media businesses.

That's true.

But then the video leaps from institutional shareholding to the claim that they own 90% of US news stations and can coordinate a nationwide campaign to manipulate Nvidia's stock.

Where is the evidence?

Which stations?

Which ownership records?

Which editorial instructions?

Which communications?

Which transactions establish the alleged scheme?

**A serious accusation requires serious evidence. The phrase "connect the dots" is not a substitute for producing it.**

Investment managers frequently hold shares through funds managed on behalf of clients.

Being a significant shareholder in a media company does not automatically mean controlling its newsroom.

And being a shareholder in Nvidia does not automatically mean having an incentive or the ability to coordinate fraudulent market manipulation.

Could institutional investors influence corporate governance?

Certainly.

Could large investors take advantage of price declines?

Absolutely.

Could financial markets experience manipulation?

Of course.

But none of those possibilities establishes this particular allegation.

And the 90% media-control figure is not substantiated by the available evidence.

Here's what fascinates me.

**The video criticises media manipulation while using a dramatic, unsupported media-manipulation claim to influence how its own audience thinks about a stock.**

What a remarkable contradiction.

### 5. Nvidia is making extraordinary money. But a great business is not automatically a great investment.

Now let's examine the actual business.

Because beneath the scandal is a genuinely remarkable financial story.

In August 2026, Nvidia reported quarterly revenue of $96.2 billion.

That's a 106% increase compared with the same quarter a year earlier.

Its data-centre business generated approximately $89 billion during the quarter.

The company reported GAAP net income of nearly $59.7 billion.

Those are extraordinary figures.

And there's genuine evidence of continuing demand.

Amazon Web Services and Nvidia announced plans in August to deploy an additional two million Nvidia GPUs across AWS infrastructure during 2027 and 2028.

Microsoft, Google and other major technology companies continue investing heavily in AI infrastructure.

So yes, Nvidia has substantial commercial momentum.

But here's the question.

**Who said a company making extraordinary profits cannot still be overpriced?**

Imagine buying an excellent business for a price that assumes its profits will grow spectacularly for years.

Now imagine investors begin questioning whether those assumptions are sustainable.

The business might continue making enormous profits.

Its revenue might keep increasing.

Its products might remain essential.

And its stock could still fall.

Why?

Because share prices reflect expectations about future earnings, risk and the returns investors demand—not simply the amount of revenue being generated today.

A stock trading at a demanding valuation can decline when expectations become less optimistic, even without a collapse in current profitability.

And that is one of the biggest misunderstandings I see among retail investors.

They assume good financial results automatically deserve a rising share price.

Not necessarily.

**The market doesn't only ask whether a company is doing well. It asks whether the company is doing well enough to justify what investors are already paying.**

Those are two different questions.

### 6. The stock is cheaper. But cheaper compared with what?

This is the part of the video designed to excite investors.

Nvidia has fallen dramatically.

Its valuation multiples have compressed.

Therefore, the stock is supposedly the cheapest it has been in a decade.

But what exactly does *cheapest* mean?

Cheapest share price?

Cheapest relative to historical earnings?

Cheapest relative to expected future earnings?

Cheapest relative to free cash flow?

These measurements can produce very different answers.

Financial analysts have highlighted how sharply Nvidia's forward price-to-earnings multiple has compressed. One November 16 market analysis placed it around 17.5 times projected earnings.

But forward earnings are estimates.

They depend on assumptions about future sales, profit margins, competition and business conditions.

If those expectations change, the apparently attractive valuation changes with them.

Let me explain.

Suppose you buy a company at 18 times expected earnings.

Looks reasonable.

But what happens if the earnings forecast turns out to be 30% too optimistic?

If the share price stays unchanged, your effective multiple against the revised earnings becomes approximately 25.7 times.

Suddenly, the supposed bargain doesn't look quite as cheap.

That's why investors need to examine the assumptions beneath valuation ratios.

**A stock isn't cheap simply because its price has fallen. It's cheap only if the price is attractive relative to a defensible estimate of its underlying value.**

And nobody knows that value with absolute certainty.

### 7. The AI revolution can be real, and investors can still lose money betting on it.

This is another uncomfortable truth.

I believe AI is transforming the economy.

Businesses are adopting it.

Developers are building with it.

Companies are investing enormous amounts into infrastructure.

And Nvidia is a major supplier of the computing technology supporting that expansion.

But none of that automatically means Nvidia's stock must keep appreciating.

An industry can transform civilisation while delivering disappointing financial returns to investors who bought its companies at excessive valuations.

History has repeatedly demonstrated that technological importance and shareholder profitability are not identical.

The internet changed the world.

Yet many investors who bought technology companies during the dot-com bubble suffered enormous losses.

The technology wasn't imaginary.

Many of the expectations embedded in stock prices were simply too optimistic.

Could something similar happen in parts of the AI industry?

It's possible.

That doesn't mean all AI investments are speculative nonsense.

It means we should examine how much actual economic value the investment is producing relative to the capital being committed.

And this is where the Nvidia conversation becomes more interesting.

How long can hyperscale technology companies continue increasing capital expenditure?

How much revenue will AI services need to generate to justify their infrastructure costs?

How quickly will AI hardware become obsolete?

Will competition place pressure on Nvidia's margins?

How will export restrictions affect future opportunities?

Can the power grids, cooling systems and construction supply chains support the planned expansion?

And how much future growth is already reflected in Nvidia's price?

**The AI revolution may be unstoppable. Your investment losses are not.**

Both statements can be true.

### 8. Texas is a real concern. But turning the issue into a political cartoon helps nobody.

The video also discusses Texas.

Here, there is a genuine development worth examining.

Gina Hinojosa, a Democrat, defeated Republican Governor Greg Abbott in the November 3, 2026 election.

Her victory represents a substantial change in Texas politics.

And data-centre policy is one of the areas where that change could matter.

Hinojosa has proposed extending a pause on new data-centre approvals until stronger protections are established.

Her proposals include protecting water availability, reducing the risk of electricity costs being passed to residents, changing tax incentives, requiring union labour and increasing transparency.

Some previously authorised projects may continue, but the future permitting environment remains uncertain.

These are meaningful considerations for infrastructure investors.

But the video frames tighter requirements primarily as annoying obstacles to technological progress.

I find that interpretation rather convenient.

**Why should ordinary households automatically absorb higher electricity costs so that trillion-dollar technology companies can expand their AI infrastructure?**

Why should communities surrender water resources without meaningful scrutiny?

Why should public authorities ignore environmental consequences simply because a project has AI attached to its name?

And on the other hand, regulators must also examine whether unnecessary delays, excessive costs or poorly designed rules could damage economic development.

There are legitimate interests on both sides.

This isn't simply a battle between people who love technology and people who hate it.

It's about distributing the benefits and costs of technological expansion.

And investors should care.

Because regulation affects project timelines, operating costs, demand expectations and ultimately earnings.

**Technology doesn't operate outside economics simply because the technology is revolutionary.**

### 9. The greatest risk may be the story investors are desperate to believe.

Now let me return to the psychological problem.

Imagine you've always wanted to own Nvidia.

You watched the stock rise.

You missed your opportunity.

You felt frustrated.

Then it crashes.

Suddenly, someone appears with a compelling explanation.

The decline isn't really about fundamentals.

The big institutions are creating panic.

The company is still incredibly profitable.

The stock is historically cheap.

And the speaker is investing $170,000.

What happens inside your mind?

You begin seeing the crash as an extraordinary opportunity.

You start imagining what your money could become when the stock returns to its previous high.

You feel that you're finally getting the chance you missed.

And now, because the video has suggested a hidden institutional conspiracy, you may even feel intellectually superior to investors who are selling.

**You're no longer merely considering an investment. You're participating in a story in which you are one of the few people clever enough to recognise the opportunity.**

That's dangerous.

Because the moment your investment decision becomes attached to your identity, contrary evidence begins feeling like a personal attack.

You stop asking whether your original assumptions are still valid.

You start defending the position.

And every further decline becomes additional evidence that powerful people are trying to frighten you out of your shares.

That kind of thinking can make almost any outcome fit the theory.

The stock rises?

The conspiracy worked.

The stock falls?

The institutions are still accumulating.

The stock remains depressed?

They're keeping the price down.

**An explanation that can accommodate every possible outcome without ever being tested is not a reliable investment thesis.**

It's a belief system.

And belief systems can become expensive when they're financed with borrowed money or life savings.

### 10. The $170,000 position isn't proof. It's part of the persuasion.

One feature of the video deserves particular scrutiny.

The narrator reveals a substantial personal investment in Nvidia.

He says he has a $170,000 position and has added $40,000 after the decline.

Then he directs viewers towards a trading community.

Now, it's perfectly reasonable for commentators to disclose their positions.

In fact, transparency about financial interests is valuable.

But disclosure doesn't automatically make the argument correct.

A person can invest a large amount of money and still be wrong.

A person can sincerely believe a stock is undervalued and still misinterpret the risks.

And someone who benefits from attracting subscribers or trading-community members has an additional commercial incentive worth recognising.

**The size of another person's investment tells you how much they've put at risk. It does not tell you whether their thesis is sound.**

And there's something else.

A $40,000 addition might be a relatively modest portfolio allocation for somebody managing millions.

For another person, $40,000 might represent their entire financial security.

Copying the transaction without understanding the investor's broader financial position is irresponsible.

You don't know the person's complete portfolio.

You don't know their cash reserves.

You don't know their hedges.

You don't know their obligations.

You don't know how much of a loss they can comfortably withstand.

So why should their investment amount become evidence that you should follow them?

**Conviction is not competence. And somebody else's appetite for risk is not your financial plan.**

### 11. So, is Nvidia actually a buying opportunity?

Here's where I refuse to join either camp.

I won't claim Nvidia is an obvious bargain.

And I won't declare that the AI bubble has burst.

Both conclusions require more evidence than a viral video can provide.

What I can say is that there are defensible reasons for optimism.

Nvidia has demonstrated exceptional revenue growth, strong profitability, an influential technological platform and substantial demand from major customers.

Its reduced valuation may offer an opportunity if future earnings meet or exceed sufficiently realistic expectations.

But there are also serious reasons for caution.

The founder-CEO has departed.

Governance concerns remain.

The company faces export restrictions, competition, infrastructure constraints, dependence on large customers and uncertainty surrounding the future economics of AI investment.

Its own financial filings acknowledge customer concentration. In the first half of fiscal 2027, three direct customers individually accounted for 16%, 15% and 13% of revenue.

That's important.

A company dependent on very large customers can experience significant consequences if purchasing patterns change.

And although Amazon's additional two million GPUs are part of an announced deployment plan, a plan is not the same as completed installations and realised revenue.

The next earnings announcement, scheduled for November 17, will provide further information.

So, rather than asking simply whether Nvidia is cheap, I would ask:

What assumptions about future earnings justify today's valuation?

How much growth is already priced in?

How would the investment perform if margins decline?

What happens if major customers slow spending?

How much could I lose if my optimistic assumptions are wrong?

And can I financially withstand that outcome?

These aren't glamorous questions.

They won't necessarily attract millions of views.

But they are considerably more useful than announcing that Wall Street is deliberately creating an opportunity for you.

### 12. Perhaps the real manipulation is the manipulation of your expectations.

I've been thinking about the entire video, and I've reached a conclusion.

I cannot prove that large institutions are manipulating Nvidia's stock through coordinated media coverage.

The evidence presented doesn't establish that.

But I can identify something else occurring right in front of us.

A financial story is being packaged to provoke emotion.

Fear of missing out.

Suspicion of institutions.

Admiration for a wealthy investor.

Excitement about a supposed bargain.

And the seductive belief that ordinary investors are about to outsmart Wall Street.

All wrapped inside a video that ultimately promotes access to the narrator's trading community.

Maybe the narrator genuinely believes everything he says.

I can't determine his intentions.

But the structure of the argument deserves scrutiny.

**A financial story doesn't have to be deliberately dishonest to become dangerously misleading.**

Sometimes the creator is simply overconfident.

Sometimes the analysis is incomplete.

Sometimes coincidence is mistaken for causation.

And sometimes the desire to produce compelling content becomes stronger than the obligation to communicate uncertainty.

That is why financial literacy requires more than understanding candlestick charts.

It requires understanding evidence.

Probability.

Valuation.

Risk.

Incentives.

And the extraordinary ability of human beings to believe stories that support decisions they've already decided they want to make.

### My final thought: don't let somebody else's exciting story become your expensive mistake.

Nvidia may recover.

It may produce extraordinary future returns.

It may remain a central beneficiary of the AI revolution for years.

Or it may experience further declines as investors reassess its earnings prospects, leadership, competition and valuation.

Nobody can guarantee the outcome.

But here's what I find increasingly disturbing about financial content.

**We've created an environment where a confident explanation is often more persuasive than a carefully supported one.**

The person who admits uncertainty appears weak.

The person who announces a hidden conspiracy appears informed.

The person who asks difficult questions appears indecisive.

And the person who confidently tells you that a stock is about to explode appears knowledgeable.

Yet investing should not be a competition to see who can tell the most exciting story.

It's the allocation of real capital under uncertainty.

Money people worked to earn.

Money families may depend on.

Money that might otherwise support businesses, education, housing or long-term financial security.

And that deserves more respect than a collection of dramatic charts and suspicious coincidences.

I don't believe retail investors should blindly trust large institutions.

But neither should they blindly trust people who build an audience by claiming those institutions control everything.

Scepticism should work in both directions.

Question Wall Street.

Question financial journalists.

Question corporate executives.

Question market analysts.

And, for goodness' sake, **question the man who says he's exposing a conspiracy while inviting you to join his paid trading community.**

Because powerful institutions can have incentives that conflict with yours.

And so can online financial influencers.

Perhaps the most important skill an investor can develop isn't predicting what a stock will do next.

It's recognising when an attractive narrative is beginning to replace serious thinking.

So here's the uncomfortable question I'm leaving in The Crazy Journal:

**ARE YOU BUYING NVIDIA BECAUSE YOU UNDERSTAND THE BUSINESS AND ITS VALUATION, OR BECAUSE SOMEBODY HAS CONVINCED YOU THAT YOU'RE SMARTER THAN EVERYONE WHO IS SELLING?**

Those are two very different reasons.

And if the stock falls another 30%, only one of them might help you reassess your decision rationally.

Because a good business can be overpriced.

A falling stock can fall further.

A conspiracy theory can be emotionally satisfying without being supported by evidence.

And a millionaire showing you his portfolio does not mean he understands your financial circumstances.

Nvidia's future will be shaped by what happens to its business, leadership, customers, competitors, regulation and the expectations investors attach to them.

Not by how passionately somebody explains a chart on social media.

**The stock market doesn't owe you a recovery simply because you've found a convincing reason for the crash.**

And perhaps the most dangerous sentence in investing is not:

*"This stock is falling."*

It's:

**"I know exactly why it's falling, and that means I cannot be wrong about what happens next."**

That's how a financial opinion becomes financial arrogance.

And financial arrogance has a remarkable ability to destroy money.
