# I THINK TRADING IS GAMBLING. THE CHARTS JUST MAKE IT LOOK INTELLIGENT.

*An uncomfortable confession about money, probability, the illusion of control and the dangerous obsession with financial freedom.*

**The Crazy Journal | November 2026**

> Risking money on an uncertain future outcome, hoping to receive more money in return: calculation does not remove gambling from an activity, it simply makes it calculated. A chart does not transform uncertainty into certainty.

I've been thinking about something for a while now, and I've finally reached a conclusion that might offend a lot of traders.

**I think trading is gambling.**

Yes, I said it.

Forex. Binary options. Synthetic indices. Day trading. Speculating on price movements.

Different instruments. Different structures. Different levels of risk.

But when you strip away the sophisticated terminology, technical indicators, chart patterns and motivational speeches, what are many retail traders actually doing?

**Risking money on an uncertain future outcome, hoping to receive more money in return.**

Sounds familiar?

Of course, there are important distinctions. A business hedging its currency exposure isn't doing the same thing as someone taking a highly leveraged trade on EUR/USD. Neither is long-term ownership of productive assets identical to short-term speculation.

I'm talking about the activity many ordinary people have been sold as their escape route from financial struggle.

The kind where someone deposits $100, opens a chart and begins dreaming about financial independence.

And before the trading community comes for my head, let me explain something.

**I didn't say trading requires no skill. I said skill doesn't automatically remove gambling from an activity.**

That distinction has completely changed how I think about trading.

## I. THE MOST SOPHISTICATED GAMBLER IS STILL TAKING A GAMBLE

People often defend trading by saying it involves analysis.

Really?

So does professional gambling.

Serious poker players study probability, expected value, psychology, bankroll management and their opponents' behaviour.

Sports bettors analyse statistics, team performance, injuries and historical results.

Even casinos calculate probabilities meticulously.

Calculation doesn't automatically make an uncertain financial wager something other than gambling.

It simply makes it calculated.

And perhaps that's where our self-deception begins.

A trader identifies support and resistance, checks moving averages, studies price action, considers market structure and enters a position.

Wonderful.

But what happens next?

They don't know.

They have a hypothesis.

They may even have a statistically supported advantage.

But the outcome remains uncertain.

**Your analysis may improve your odds. It does not eliminate the possibility that you're wrong.**

And here's the part many people don't want to hear:

You can make the correct decision and lose money.

You can make a terrible decision and win money.

The market doesn't issue rewards according to how intelligently you behaved.

That is why winning a trade is not proof that you made a good decision.

And losing one isn't necessarily proof that you made a bad one.

So why do we keep treating a green trade like a certificate of intelligence?

## II. TRADING HAS AN EXTRAORDINARY ABILITY TO MAKE YOU FEEL INTELLIGENT WHILE YOU'RE LOSING MONEY

This is the part I find personally disturbing.

I know what it means to understand risk management intellectually and still struggle with the emotional consequences of losing.

You create rules.

You establish limits.

You tell yourself that you're disciplined.

Then a trade goes against you.

Suddenly, the problem isn't the market anymore.

It's your ego.

You don't want to accept that you were wrong.

You don't want to end the session at a loss.

You don't want to walk away knowing the money is gone.

So you convince yourself there's another opportunity.

One more setup.

One more confirmation.

One more chance to recover.

And if you eventually recover the money, something dangerous happens.

**The market rewards your indiscipline, and you mistake the recovery for evidence that your behaviour was justified.**

That is a terrifying psychological trap.

Because now the next time you lose, your brain remembers the recovery rather than the danger.

You begin to believe that persistence will eventually rescue you.

But markets are not obligated to reward persistence.

And when persistence becomes an inability to stop, what exactly separates your behaviour from that of someone chasing losses in a casino?

The chart?

The candlesticks?

The fact that you call your wager a position?

Come on.

**Changing the vocabulary doesn't necessarily change the behaviour.**

## III. THE PROBLEM ISN'T THAT TRADING CANNOT MAKE YOU RICH: MISTAKING POSSIBILITY FOR A PLAN

I can already hear the objections.

"But people have made millions trading!"

Of course.

People have also made millions playing poker.

People have won lotteries.

Some people have built extraordinary careers as professional speculators.

Their success doesn't automatically establish that the same path is financially sensible for everybody else.

This is where I think we're intellectually dishonest with ourselves.

We take an exceptional outcome and turn it into an ordinary expectation.

We see one successful trader and ignore the thousands who never achieved anything close.

We see the withdrawal screenshot but never ask for the complete deposit history.

We celebrate a trader who turned $500 into $10,000 but never investigate what happened to the account six months later.

And then we tell ourselves:

*If they can do it, I can do it.*

Maybe.

But what is the probability?

That's the question that should matter.

Researchers examining Taiwanese day traders found that fewer than 1% of their studied population demonstrated reliably profitable performance above the benchmark after fees.

Australia's financial regulator reported that 68% of retail CFD investors lost money in its 2024 financial year.

These figures don't prove nobody can succeed. They don't describe every market or every trader.

But they demolish the fantasy that profitability is merely a matter of finding the right indicator.

**A success story tells you what happened to one person. It doesn't tell you your chances of repeating it.**

And if you're building your financial future around exceptions, you'd better understand how expensive that assumption could become.

## IV. I NO LONGER BELIEVE TRADING SHOULD BE SOLD AS A ROAD TO WEALTH

This might be my most controversial conclusion.

**Trading can generate income. Trading can increase capital. But for most ordinary people, I don't consider active speculation a dependable wealth-building strategy.**

Why?

Because building wealth and making money are not the same thing.

You can make $1,000 today and lose $1,500 tomorrow.

You can end one month in extraordinary profit and spend the next three months attempting to recover a drawdown.

You can appear successful while your overall financial position is deteriorating.

And even if you are profitable, you must confront a simple economic reality.

Small capital produces small absolute returns unless you take substantial risks or achieve unusually high returns.

A person with $500 who earns 2% in a month has made $10 before costs.

Ten dollars.

But suppose that person needs $500 monthly to survive.

What happens?

They start chasing returns their capital cannot reasonably support.

Now risk management becomes an inconvenience.

Leverage becomes attractive.

Patience becomes frustrating.

And the account becomes a battlefield between financial reality and personal desperation.

**The problem may not be your trading strategy. It may be that you're demanding an impossible lifestyle from the capital you possess.**

Wealth, to me, is something much bigger than a profitable trading session.

It's productive assets.

Reliable income.

Ownership.

Financial reserves.

The ability to survive unexpected events.

The freedom to make decisions without being financially desperate.

And, importantly, the ability to keep what you've accumulated.

A trading account can contribute to those things for a skilled and appropriately capitalised person.

But it doesn't automatically create them.

## V. COMPOUNDING IS BEAUTIFUL MATHEMATICS: THE MARKET DOESN'T READ YOUR SPREADSHEET

Oh, how we love compounding.

Take $100.

Assume a certain percentage of profit every day.

Extend the calculation for one year.

And suddenly, your spreadsheet tells you that you're on your way to becoming a millionaire.

Fantastic.

But you've quietly inserted an assumption into your calculation.

**That the returns will keep coming.**

Your spreadsheet doesn't experience losing streaks.

It doesn't get emotional.

It doesn't suffer slippage, transaction costs or an unexpected market shock.

It doesn't panic when half its capital disappears.

Real trading does.

Compounding works when returns accumulate. But losses compound as well.

If you lose 50% of your capital, you need a 100% gain on what remains just to recover.

And if the underlying strategy loses money on average, compounding doesn't rescue it.

It can accelerate the destruction of the account.

So yes, you can compound trading profits.

But **compounding is not evidence that you possess a profitable strategy.**

It's arithmetic.

The difficult part is consistently generating sustainable returns without taking risks that eventually wipe you out.

And that's the part the millionaire calculators conveniently leave out.

## VI. THE MOST EXPENSIVE PART OF TRADING MIGHT NOT EVEN BE THE MONEY YOU LOSE

I've started thinking about something we rarely calculate.

Opportunity cost.

Suppose you spend three years trying to become consistently profitable.

Three years studying charts.

Three years backtesting strategies.

Three years watching sessions open and close.

Three years experiencing the emotional highs and lows of wins and losses.

Now imagine that after those three years, your net financial result is close to zero.

Or worse, negative.

What else could you have built in those three years?

A valuable professional skill?

A profitable business?

A meaningful career?

An income-producing product?

A stronger financial foundation?

I'm not suggesting everybody who studies trading is wasting their time.

Learning about markets can be valuable.

But there's a difference between learning about financial markets and dedicating your productive years to an activity that has not demonstrated the ability to reward you.

**Sometimes the most expensive thing you're losing isn't money. It's the alternative future you could have been building.**

And this is what bothers me most about the trading dream.

We often measure losses in dollars.

But rarely in years.

## VII. MAYBE THE REAL ADDICTION ISN'T TRADING: THE ILLUSION OF SUDDEN TRANSFORMATION

Why does trading attract so many people?

I don't think everyone is fascinated by financial markets.

Some people simply want to stop being poor.

They want independence.

They want to escape frustrating jobs.

They want to support their families.

They want the freedom to live differently.

There's absolutely nothing wrong with those desires.

But trading offers something psychologically intoxicating.

The possibility that everything could change very quickly.

A few correct predictions.

A few successful sessions.

A few compounded returns.

And suddenly, the ordinary struggle of building wealth appears unnecessary.

**Trading can sell you a fantasy in which patience is optional and financial freedom is only a few correct decisions away.**

And once you've tasted a large win, that fantasy becomes difficult to abandon.

Not necessarily because the strategy is working.

But because you've seen what *could* happen.

Perhaps that's why some people continue trading long after their own records suggest they should stop.

They're not necessarily attached to the activity.

They're attached to the imagined future.

And giving up trading feels like giving up that future.

But those are not the same thing.

**Abandoning one route to wealth does not mean abandoning the possibility of becoming wealthy.**

That distinction might save somebody years of unnecessary struggle.

## VIII. SO, AM I SAYING EVERYBODY SHOULD QUIT TRADING?

No.

That would be intellectually lazy.

There are skilled traders. There are professional trading firms. There are legitimate commercial reasons to trade financial instruments, and some speculative strategies can have positive expected returns.

But I think we need to stop treating trading as a universal opportunity for financial freedom.

If you're consistently profitable, can independently verify your results, understand your risks and have the capital to withstand losses without destabilising your life, your circumstances are different.

But if you've been trading for years without a credible positive net return, why are you still doing it?

If you repeatedly break your rules, why do you believe another strategy will fix the problem?

If you keep depositing money to recover losses, what evidence suggests that the next deposit will be different?

And if your trading activity is consuming your savings, peace of mind or important relationships, at what point do you admit that the price has become too high?

**There is no shame in deciding that an opportunity is not worth its cost.**

Not every mountain deserves to be climbed.

Not every competition deserves your participation.

And not every possible way of making money deserves to become your financial strategy.

Sometimes walking away is a better decision than becoming exceptionally skilled at surviving something that keeps damaging you.

## IX. PERHAPS WE NEED TO STOP TRYING TO TRADE OUR WAY OUT OF POVERTY

This is the thought I keep returning to.

What if our obsession with trading is distracting us from more dependable ways of building wealth?

Developing valuable skills.

Creating businesses.

Solving problems people willingly pay to have solved.

Increasing income.

Saving consistently.

Building ownership in productive assets.

Investing over time.

None guarantees wealth. Businesses fail. Careers collapse. Investments lose value.

But these routes don't necessarily require you to repeatedly risk your available capital on short-term price predictions.

And unlike speculation, some of them can create value beyond the outcome of a market movement.

A business can serve customers.

A skill can increase what people are willing to pay you.

A productive asset can generate earnings.

A diversified investment portfolio can participate in the long-term growth of underlying businesses, while still carrying market risk.

Those are different economic mechanisms.

And I think the distinction matters.

**Maybe we have become so obsessed with making money move that we've forgotten how to create value.**

We want a trading account that pays us $100 every day.

But we're unwilling to spend months building something that solves a $100 problem for somebody.

We want financial freedom in ninety days.

But refuse to confront the reality that meaningful wealth often requires years of consistent effort.

And because the slower road looks boring, we convince ourselves there must be a shortcut.

Sometimes there isn't.

Sometimes the boring road is the road.

## X. THE CONCLUSION I NEVER EXPECTED TO REACH

I started thinking about trading as an opportunity.

I studied the strategies, the mathematics, the discipline and the psychology.

And somewhere along the way, I began questioning the fundamental premise.

What exactly am I trying to achieve?

Am I trying to become a skilled market speculator?

Or am I trying to become financially independent?

Because those are not identical goals.

You can become obsessed with mastering trading while making very little progress towards financial independence.

You can know everything about charts and almost nothing about building assets.

You can be profitable for several weeks and remain financially insecure.

And you can become so emotionally invested in recovering trading losses that you forget why you started looking for financial freedom in the first place.

That is the paradox that troubles me.

**You can spend years chasing financial freedom through trading and become less financially free because of trading.**

I'm not saying every trader is a gambler in the same sense, or that the markets are inherently fraudulent.

I'm saying that when I examine short-term retail speculation honestly, I see a calculated wager on uncertainty.

Sometimes a well-researched wager.

Sometimes a poorly understood one.

Sometimes one with a genuine mathematical advantage.

And sometimes one powered entirely by hope.

But I refuse to confuse the sophistication of the process with the certainty of the outcome.

A chart doesn't transform uncertainty into certainty.

An indicator doesn't guarantee an advantage.

A stop-loss doesn't automatically make a strategy profitable.

And calling yourself a trader doesn't exempt you from the psychological dangers of gambling.

## XI. THE FINAL BREAKTHROUGH: WHY DO YOU STILL WANT TO BE A TRADER?

I don't know whether this conclusion will offend professional traders.

Perhaps it will.

I don't particularly care.

I'm not writing this to insult people who trade successfully. Nor am I claiming that my personal experiences establish a universal law.

I'm writing because I've become increasingly suspicious of any activity that promises financial liberation while repeatedly putting people's existing financial security at risk.

I'm also questioning my own assumptions.

Why was I attracted to trading?

Was it the intellectual challenge?

Was it a genuine statistical advantage?

Or was it the possibility of making money much faster than conventional work appeared to allow?

And if I removed the possibility of extraordinary short-term returns, would trading still occupy such an important place in my thinking?

That's a difficult question.

Because sometimes we don't want to admit that we're not pursuing an opportunity because it makes sense.

We're pursuing it because we desperately want it to work.

**And desperation has an extraordinary ability to disguise itself as determination.**

I've reached a point where I believe financial freedom should be measured by what you own, what you earn, what you can sustain and how much control you have over your life.

Not how many winning trades you took this week.

Not how impressive your trading dashboard looks.

Not how much money you nearly made.

And certainly not how much you believe you'll recover tomorrow.

Maybe trading can be profitable.

Maybe a few people will build substantial wealth through it.

But I no longer believe the average person should treat speculative trading as their primary road to wealth.

It's too uncertain, too easy to misuse and too unforgiving of the human weaknesses we all carry.

So here's my final, uncomfortable question:

**IF YOU HAD NEVER DISCOVERED TRADING, WOULD YOU ACTUALLY BE POORER TODAY?**

Think about the money.

Think about the time.

Think about the opportunities.

Think about the emotional energy.

And think about the person you might have become if all those resources had been directed somewhere else.

If the answer makes you uncomfortable, perhaps the question isn't whether you can still become a successful trader.

Perhaps the question is why you're still so determined to become one.

**Because maybe the greatest financial breakthrough you will ever experience is not learning how to win another trade.**

**Maybe it's finally accepting that you don't need to trade to become wealthy.**

And perhaps you never did.
