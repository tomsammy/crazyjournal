# STOP PROMISING PEOPLE HEAVEN WHILE HELPING TO MAKE THEIR LIVES HELL ON EARTH.

*The uncomfortable hypocrisy of a Christianity that wants to save your soul but has very little interest in your suffering.*

**The Crazy Journal | November 2026**

> What if the greatest failure of modern Christianity isn't that we don't preach Jesus enough, but that we have become very good at preaching a Jesus whose way of life we are unwilling to follow? We tell hungry people that Jesus loves them, tell struggling families that God will provide, and tell the oppressed that their suffering is temporary—then return to our comfortable homes and congratulate ourselves for spreading the gospel. A promise of heaven should never become an excuse for making peace with hell on earth.

![Stop Promising People Heaven While Helping to Make Their Lives Hell on Earth](/images/journals/heaven-and-hell-on-earth.jpg)

**What if the greatest failure of modern Christianity isn't that we don't preach Jesus enough, but that we have become very good at preaching a Jesus whose way of life we are unwilling to follow?**

I know.

That sounds offensive.

Especially to people who believe that preaching the gospel is the highest expression of Christian responsibility.

But I've been thinking about something, and the more I examine it, the more uncomfortable I become.

We tell hungry people that Jesus loves them.

We tell struggling families that God will provide.

We tell the oppressed that their suffering is temporary.

We tell people living under terrible conditions that their reward is waiting in heaven.

Then we return to our comfortable homes, eat our meals, lock our doors and congratulate ourselves for spreading the gospel.

**What a convenient religion.**

A religion that allows me to feel spiritually accomplished without necessarily becoming materially inconvenient to anyone else's suffering.

A religion that promises people eternal happiness while asking them to tolerate present injustice.

A religion in which I can be passionately concerned about your soul and remarkably indifferent to the conditions destroying your life.

And perhaps the most disturbing part?

We have become so accustomed to this contradiction that we sometimes mistake it for faithfulness.

### 1. Did Jesus really come to earth simply to organise our departure from it?

Let's begin with the question that has been troubling me.

What exactly is the purpose of Christianity?

Is the entire objective to get people saved, keep them morally acceptable and eventually transport them to heaven?

Is the earth simply a temporary examination hall where human beings are expected to endure suffering until their results are announced in eternity?

Because if that's the complete story, something about the biblical narrative becomes difficult to explain.

In Genesis, humanity is placed within creation.

Human beings are given responsibilities involving stewardship, cultivation, relationships and the care of the world around them.

The story doesn't begin with people trying to escape the earth.

It begins with God creating a world and calling it good.

Then, in the New Testament, something extraordinary happens.

God doesn't simply send an instruction manual explaining how human beings can escape their physical existence.

**God comes into the world.**

The Christian claim of the incarnation is precisely that God enters human history in Jesus Christ.

And Jesus doesn't spend his ministry merely distributing tickets to heaven.

He encounters sick people.

Hungry people.

Excluded people.

People whose humanity has been diminished by the social and religious structures around them.

He heals.

He feeds.

He welcomes.

He confronts hypocrisy.

He challenges the abuse of religious authority.

He announces the kingdom of God.

Now, heaven and eternal life are undeniably part of Christian teaching. Jesus speaks about both. The Christian hope also includes resurrection and the renewal of creation.

But that makes the reduction of Christianity to *escaping earth* even more questionable.

Because the biblical picture is not simply humanity abandoning a ruined world.

Revelation 21 describes a renewed creation and God's dwelling with humanity.

**So why have some of us reduced a message of restoration into an elaborate evacuation programme?**

Why have we made Christianity sound as though God is interested in extracting souls from the world but not in confronting the suffering within it?

That's a theological question worth taking seriously.

### 2. We have turned prayer into a respectable excuse for doing nothing.

Before anyone misunderstands me, I believe prayer matters deeply within Christian faith.

But I also believe we have sometimes abused it.

Someone tells you they haven't eaten.

You say, "God will provide."

Someone cannot afford medical treatment.

You say, "It is well."

A widow is struggling to care for her children.

You say, "The Lord is your strength."

A young person is being exploited by an employer.

You say, "Keep praying. God will fight for you."

Wonderful.

But what if God expects you to do something?

What if the provision that person needs could partly come through your generosity?

What if the widow needs financial assistance, not another motivational prophecy?

What if the exploited worker needs someone willing to speak against injustice?

**What if your prayer is sincere, but your refusal to act is not?**

James 2:15–17 directly confronts the emptiness of offering good wishes to someone lacking food and clothing without providing practical help.

It's a striking passage.

Because it refuses to separate the language of faith from the responsibility of love.

Yet we've become experts at precisely that separation.

We organise prayer meetings for problems we have the resources to address.

We announce fasting programmes while ignoring people who are hungry because they cannot afford food.

We ask God to bless the poor while refusing to question the conditions that keep them poor.

And when challenged, we remind everybody that our responsibility is spiritual.

**But since when did spiritual responsibility become permission to abandon physical human beings?**

Prayer should not become the substitute for compassion when compassion is within our power.

Sometimes prayer should move us towards action.

And sometimes the most powerful response to someone's suffering is not another sentence beginning with "I decree."

It's asking:

"What do you actually need, and what can I realistically do to help?"

### 3. Jesus fed people. We sometimes feed them religious explanations for their hunger.

There's something almost embarrassing about the contrast.

In the Gospels, Jesus feeds hungry crowds.

In Matthew 25, he identifies care for hungry, thirsty, sick, imprisoned and vulnerable people with service rendered to him.

In Luke 4, Jesus announces his mission in language associated with good news for the poor, release for captives and liberation for the oppressed.

Yet somehow, we have developed versions of Christianity in which addressing material suffering is treated as secondary, optional or suspiciously political.

How?

How did we manage to move from the example of Jesus to a religious culture in which helping people meet basic needs can appear less important than organising another spectacular programme?

And don't tell me that the gospel is not about food.

Of course, it isn't *only* about food.

But the people Jesus fed were not imaginary stomachs.

They were human beings.

Bodies.

Needs.

Histories.

Relationships.

Circumstances.

**You cannot claim to love humanity while treating the material conditions of human existence as spiritually irrelevant.**

A child who goes to bed hungry doesn't become less hungry because you've correctly explained the doctrine of salvation.

A patient who cannot afford treatment doesn't stop suffering because you've reminded them that heaven has no sickness.

A person facing discrimination doesn't automatically regain dignity because you've promised them a future reward.

The Christian message of eternal hope can be meaningful in suffering.

But it becomes morally distorted when we use that hope to excuse our unwillingness to confront avoidable suffering.

**A promise of heaven should never become an excuse for making peace with hell on earth.**

### 4. Some of our churches have become very comfortable with the suffering surrounding them.

This is where I expect people to become angry.

And that's fine.

Because I think certain questions deserve to make us angry.

How can a congregation celebrate extraordinary financial achievements while remaining indifferent to severe hardship within its own community?

How can we spend enormous amounts creating impressive religious environments while treating assistance to the vulnerable as an afterthought?

How can we raise money enthusiastically for prestige projects but struggle to build transparent systems that help families facing genuine emergencies?

Now, I am not saying church buildings are inherently wrong.

I am not saying every congregation is wealthy.

And I certainly won't ignore the many churches and Christian organisations operating hospitals, schools, food banks, shelters and humanitarian programmes.

Those contributions matter.

But the existence of good work doesn't make every religious institution immune from criticism.

**A beautiful building is not automatically evidence of a beautiful ministry.**

A crowded auditorium doesn't automatically indicate transformed lives.

An expensive sound system doesn't demonstrate compassion.

And a preacher's influence doesn't prove that the people around the ministry are better cared for.

We can have excellent worship and terrible social responsibility.

We can have sound theology and deeply questionable priorities.

We can sing passionately about God's love and be remarkably unwilling to demonstrate that love when it becomes financially inconvenient.

At some point, we have to ask ourselves:

**Are we building communities that reflect the character of Christ, or institutions that reflect our obsession with religious success?**

Because the two are not necessarily the same.

### 5. We have become suspiciously good at telling the oppressed to be patient.

This one particularly bothers me.

Throughout history, religious language has sometimes been used to justify authority and encourage submission in the face of injustice.

And the possibility hasn't disappeared.

An employee is being mistreated.

We tell them to submit.

A woman is experiencing abuse.

We tell her to preserve the family at all costs.

Poor people complain about exploitation.

We remind them that their reward is in heaven.

A congregation questions financial accountability.

We warn them against challenging spiritual authority.

How convenient.

**Christianity becomes dangerously useful to oppressors when patience is preached to victims but accountability is never demanded from those causing the suffering.**

And please, don't misunderstand the argument.

Christianity teaches forgiveness.

It teaches humility.

It teaches patience.

But it also teaches justice, mercy and responsibility.

Forgiveness does not require remaining in an abusive situation.

Humility does not mean accepting exploitation.

Respecting authority does not make authority morally infallible.

And patience is not a command to remain silent forever while injustice continues.

The prophets of Israel repeatedly condemned corruption, exploitation and religious hypocrisy.

Amos 5 challenges worship that is disconnected from justice.

Micah 6:8 connects faithful living with justice, mercy and humility.

The biblical witness is hardly indifferent to how powerful people treat those with less power.

So why do we sometimes become uncomfortable when the same questions are asked of our own institutions?

**Perhaps because a gospel that confronts oppression is considerably more expensive to practise than a gospel that merely promises an escape from it.**

One requires words.

The other may require sacrifice.

### 6. We want to save souls without confronting the systems that keep destroying bodies.

I find this particularly disturbing.

Suppose a community suffers because of corrupt administration, unsafe working conditions, inadequate healthcare or exploitative economic practices.

What should Christian responsibility look like?

Should believers simply evangelise the people affected and leave the structures untouched?

Should we organise revival meetings without ever asking why preventable suffering continues?

Should we pray for people trapped in injustice while refusing to question the injustice itself?

I don't think so.

If loving your neighbour means taking their wellbeing seriously, then the forces affecting that wellbeing cannot be completely irrelevant.

That doesn't mean every Christian must become a political activist.

It doesn't mean every church should endorse a political party.

And it doesn't mean that every complicated social problem has an obvious biblical solution.

But **refusing to become partisan is not the same as refusing to care about justice.**

You can demand honesty without endorsing a politician.

You can oppose exploitation without declaring allegiance to an ideology.

You can advocate for access to healthcare, protection of vulnerable people and responsible government without reducing Christianity to partisan propaganda.

And you can disagree about the best economic or political approach while still recognising that human suffering deserves a meaningful response.

I think we need to recover that distinction.

Because when Christian faith refuses to examine systems of injustice, it can become an excellent source of comfort for those who benefit from those systems.

People are told to endure.

To wait.

To pray.

To trust that God will eventually intervene.

Meanwhile, the people responsible for the suffering continue operating without meaningful accountability.

**There is something deeply wrong with using the language of divine justice to postpone the pursuit of human justice indefinitely.**

### 7. Environmental destruction is also a theological problem.

Here's another contradiction.

We say God created the world.

We say creation reveals his glory.

We say human beings have been entrusted with responsibilities towards it.

But some of us behave as though the earth is disposable.

We pollute.

Waste.

Destroy.

Ignore environmental degradation.

Then reassure ourselves that heaven is our ultimate home.

But why should the hope of a renewed creation justify mistreating the creation that currently exists?

That logic doesn't make sense to me.

If someone entrusted you with a beautiful home and promised that one day it would be restored, would that give you permission to destroy everything inside it?

Of course not.

And yet we've sometimes treated environmental responsibility as though it were a distraction from spiritual matters.

But polluted water affects real people.

Unsafe environments affect children's health.

Environmental destruction can destroy livelihoods and worsen vulnerability.

These aren't abstract concerns.

They're questions about how our actions affect other human beings and the world we share.

**You cannot claim to love your neighbour while remaining completely indifferent to the conditions in which your neighbour must live.**

Christian stewardship should mean something beyond repeating that God owns the earth.

It should influence how we treat it.

### 8. But I also reject the idea that Christianity should be reduced to social activism.

Now let me challenge the argument from the opposite direction.

Because I don't want to exchange one incomplete gospel for another.

If Christianity becomes nothing more than feeding people, building hospitals and challenging unjust systems, we have also reduced its message.

Christianity makes profound claims about sin, grace, reconciliation with God, resurrection, forgiveness and eternal life.

Those claims cannot simply be removed because social action appears more practical.

And helping people materially is not the same thing as automatically fulfilling every dimension of Christian discipleship.

**The problem isn't that Christians preach salvation. The problem is when salvation is preached as though love, justice and human wellbeing are optional consequences.**

I don't think we should be forced to choose between evangelism and compassion.

Between prayer and practical help.

Between spiritual transformation and social responsibility.

### 9. A faith that refuses to look away from suffering.

The true test of Christian faith has never been our ability to describe heaven in poetic terms.

It is our willingness to confront hell on earth with practical compassion.

If our gospel only works when people are dead, it isn't the gospel of Jesus.

Jesus did not stand at a safe distance from human agony, delivering speeches about eternal rewards while stepping over wounded bodies on the road to Jericho.

He stopped.

He touched the leper.

He fed the hungry.

He called religious leaders hypocrites when they prioritized legalistic prestige over justice, mercy and faithfulness.

If we want to preach Jesus, we must be willing to live like him.

That means praying with our knees, but also serving with our hands.

That means offering hope for eternity, while fighting with every ounce of our strength against the conditions that make someone's present existence unbearable.

And until we are willing to do that, perhaps we should be a little more humble with our theology.

Because God is not impressed by how loudly we promise people heaven when our indifference is helping to make their lives hell on earth.
