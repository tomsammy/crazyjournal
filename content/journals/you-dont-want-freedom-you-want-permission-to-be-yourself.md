# YOU DON'T WANT FREEDOM. YOU WANT PERMISSION TO BE YOURSELF.

*The Courage to Be Disliked — and the uncomfortable truth about the lives we perform for other people's approval.*

**The Crazy Journal | November 2026**

> Who would you be if nobody was watching? What if the greatest prison we live in isn't poverty, failure or circumstance, but the exhausting obligation to remain acceptable to everybody? What a terrible achievement: becoming everybody's favourite person while becoming a stranger to yourself.

![The Courage to Be Disliked — You Don't Want Freedom. You Want Permission to Be Yourself.](/images/journals/courage-to-be-disliked.jpg)

**I have a question. And I don't think most of us will like the answer.**

Who would you be if nobody was watching?

Not your parents.

Not your friends.

Not your colleagues.

Not your church members.

Not your social media followers.

Nobody.

Would you still be pursuing that career?

Would you still dress the same way?

Would you still believe everything you publicly defend?

Would you still be living the life you're currently living?

Or would you finally admit that a significant portion of your personality has been carefully constructed to avoid disappointing people?

I've been thinking about a book titled *The Courage to Be Disliked* by Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga.

And something about its central argument makes me deeply uncomfortable.

**What if the greatest prison we live in isn't poverty, failure or circumstance, but the exhausting obligation to remain acceptable to everybody?**

What if the person you've spent years trying to become isn't actually you?

What if you've become so good at being liked that you've completely forgotten what you genuinely want?

And what if the reason you're so afraid of being disliked is that you've spent your entire life using other people's approval as evidence that you're worth something?

Now that's a disturbing thought.

## I. PROFESSIONAL MANAGERS OF OTHER PEOPLE'S OPINIONS

Let's be serious.

Many of us don't live our lives.

We manage public perception.

We make decisions while imagining how people will react.

We choose careers partly because of the respect attached to their titles.

We buy things we don't particularly need because we want to appear successful.

We remain in relationships long after they've become unhealthy because we're afraid of what people will say.

We attend events we don't want to attend.

Agree with opinions we don't believe.

Laugh at jokes we don't find funny.

And sometimes, we even pretend to be happy because explaining our unhappiness would require conversations we're not prepared to have.

**We've turned our entire existence into a public relations exercise.**

And the most ridiculous part?

The audience we're performing for is usually too busy worrying about its own reputation to pay as much attention to us as we imagine.

Think about that.

You're sacrificing your peace to impress someone who is also sacrificing their peace to impress somebody else.

An entire society of people pretending for the benefit of other people who are also pretending.

What a magnificent waste of human existence.

## II. THE TRAGEDY OF BEING LOVED FOR SOMEONE YOU'RE PRETENDING TO BE

Here's something that bothers me.

People say they want to be loved.

But what exactly do they want to be loved for?

Their actual personality?

Their genuine beliefs?

Their imperfections?

Or the carefully edited version of themselves they've spent years presenting?

Imagine spending twenty years becoming the person everyone expects.

You become the responsible child.

The agreeable friend.

The impressive professional.

The respectable member of society.

Everybody praises you.

Everybody says you're wonderful.

And somewhere inside, you're miserable.

Because all the admiration is directed towards a version of yourself you no longer recognise.

**What a terrible achievement: becoming everybody's favourite person while becoming a stranger to yourself.**

And perhaps this explains why external validation can become so addictive.

You get praised.

You feel good.

Then the feeling disappears.

So you need more praise.

More recognition.

More acceptance.

Another compliment.

Another achievement.

Another opportunity to prove that you deserve to be liked.

At what point does that end?

How many people must approve of your life before you finally grant yourself permission to live it?

## III. THE SEPARATION OF TASKS: OTHER PEOPLE'S OPINIONS ARE NOT YOUR ASSIGNMENT

One of the most fascinating ideas in *The Courage to Be Disliked* is something called the **separation of tasks**.

The book introduces this through the philosophy of Alfred Adler.

The basic question is simple:

*Whose responsibility is this?*

Suppose I make a decision after honestly considering its consequences.

I explain myself respectfully.

I act with integrity.

And somebody decides they don't like me anymore.

What exactly am I supposed to do?

I can listen to their concerns.

I can reconsider my decision if they've identified something I've overlooked.

I can apologise if I've caused harm.

But can I force them to approve of me?

No.

Their judgement belongs to them.

My conduct belongs to me.

**And perhaps one of the greatest forms of emotional maturity is learning not to take responsibility for decisions that belong to other people.**

But this is where things become difficult.

Because many of us don't simply want to make good decisions.

We want everyone to agree that our decisions are good.

We want to say no without anybody becoming disappointed.

We want to establish boundaries without anyone calling us selfish.

We want to pursue unusual ambitions without anybody questioning our judgement.

We want to become independent without ever making our parents uncomfortable.

In other words, **we don't just want freedom. We want freedom without the social consequences of exercising it.**

And perhaps that is why so many people remain trapped.

They're waiting for a permission that may never come.

## IV. A DANGEROUS MISUNDERSTANDING: BEING DISLIKED DOESN'T MAKE YOU RIGHT

This is where I refuse to turn the book into another collection of motivational nonsense.

Because I can already imagine someone reading it and announcing:

"I don't care what anybody thinks about me!"

Wonderful.

But what if you're actually a terrible person?

What if people dislike you because you're dishonest?

What if your colleagues avoid you because you're arrogant?

What if your friends are tired of your selfishness?

What if people aren't criticising you because they're threatened by your authenticity, but because your behaviour genuinely hurts them?

**Being disliked is not proof that you're courageous. Sometimes it's evidence that you're behaving badly.**

And we need to stop confusing the two.

Not every criticism is jealousy.

Not every rejection is persecution.

Not everybody who disagrees with you is intimidated by your potential.

Sometimes you're wrong.

Sometimes your attitude is the problem.

Sometimes the people challenging you are doing you an extraordinary favour.

So perhaps the real courage isn't simply accepting that people may dislike you.

**It's having enough independence to survive unfair criticism and enough humility to accept fair criticism.**

Now that's difficult.

Because one requires confidence.

The other requires self-examination.

And human beings are often very good at practising whichever one protects their ego.

## V. THE UNCOMFORTABLE TRUTH ABOUT YOUR PAST: INFLUENCE VS. DETERMINATION

There's another part of the book that I find both fascinating and problematic.

Its presentation of Adlerian psychology challenges the idea that our past must determine who we become.

That's a powerful thought.

But the book goes further, using the provocative claim that trauma doesn't exist as a determining cause of our lives.

And this is where I have to disagree with any overly literal interpretation.

Trauma is real.

Abuse is real.

Grief is real.

Some experiences leave psychological consequences that don't disappear because someone tells you to choose a different attitude.

People can develop post-traumatic stress disorder, and recovering from serious psychological harm may require treatment, support and time.

**Telling someone they have choices is empowering. Telling them their suffering is simply a choice can become cruel.**

Those are not the same thing.

However, I think there's still an important idea worth rescuing.

Our past can influence us without necessarily defining every possibility available to us.

What happened to you matters.

What was done to you matters.

But it doesn't follow that every chapter of your future has already been written.

And perhaps the real freedom is not pretending your past never happened.

It's refusing to let the worst parts of your history become the only explanation of who you are allowed to become.

That's a much more difficult, and more honest, kind of courage.

## VI. SOCIAL MEDIA AND THE EMBARRASSING HUNGER FOR APPROVAL

Imagine posting something online.

Something you genuinely believe.

You check your phone ten minutes later.

Three likes.

No comments.

Nobody shares it.

Suddenly, you're questioning whether the idea was worth expressing.

Now imagine posting something you don't particularly believe.

Perhaps an exaggerated success story.

A fashionable opinion.

A motivational statement you've seen repeated a thousand times.

It gets hundreds of reactions.

People praise you.

You feel brilliant.

So what happens next?

You begin learning which version of yourself receives applause.

And gradually, your audience becomes the editor of your personality.

You stop asking, *Is this true?*

You start asking, *Will people like this?*

You stop asking, *Do I believe this?*

You start asking, *Will this perform well?*

**And before you know it, an algorithm has become the unofficial manager of your convictions.**

I find that terrifying.

Not because wanting appreciation is wrong.

Human beings naturally value recognition and belonging.

But when approval becomes the primary condition for expressing yourself, you begin losing the ability to think independently.

You become very good at sensing the mood of the room.

And increasingly afraid of saying anything that might disturb it.

Perhaps that's why genuinely independent thinking is so uncomfortable.

It comes with the possibility of being wrong.

Being misunderstood.

Being challenged.

And sometimes being completely alone in your opinion.

## VII. THE PARADOX: THE COURAGE TO BE DISLIKED IS NOT THE DESIRE TO BE HATED

I don't want anyone misunderstanding the point.

The objective isn't to become unnecessarily offensive.

It isn't to insult everyone around you and celebrate their anger as evidence of your superiority.

It isn't to abandon community, friendship or responsibility.

In fact, one of the more interesting dimensions of the book is its emphasis on community feeling and contribution.

The argument isn't simply that other people don't matter.

It's that **your worth shouldn't depend entirely on whether other people are pleased with you.**

And that distinction is enormous.

You can love people without surrendering your judgement.

You can respect people without agreeing with everything they say.

You can be generous without permitting exploitation.

You can contribute to a community without allowing that community to dictate every decision you make.

You can be kind and still say no.

You can be honest and still be misunderstood.

You can do the right thing and still disappoint people.

And sometimes the price of acting according to your convictions is that someone who once admired you may no longer admire you.

That's painful.

But perhaps it is also part of being an independent human being.

## VIII. DANGEROUS PEOPLE-PLEASING: WHEN YOU BEGIN LYING TO YOURSELF

I don't want to write about this as though I'm standing somewhere above the problem.

I'm not.

When I examine my own decisions, I have to ask how often my judgement is influenced by how I want to be perceived.

Am I pursuing something because it genuinely matters to me?

Or because I want to be recognised for pursuing it?

Do I sometimes explain my achievements in ways designed to impress rather than simply inform?

Have I ever avoided saying something I genuinely believed because I didn't want the discomfort of disagreement?

And when someone criticises me, am I really evaluating their argument?

Or am I simply trying to protect the image I have of myself?

Those questions are uncomfortable.

Because the desire to be liked doesn't always arrive as obvious insecurity.

Sometimes it disguises itself as professionalism.

Respect.

Humility.

Maturity.

Even kindness.

And of course, these qualities can be genuine.

But sometimes we're not being peaceful.

We're avoiding necessary confrontation.

Sometimes we're not being humble.

We're afraid of being visible.

Sometimes we're not being considerate.

We're terrified of disappointing people.

**And sometimes what we proudly call good character is simply fear that has learnt excellent manners.**

I think that possibility deserves serious reflection.

## IX. WHAT WOULD ACTUALLY CHANGE IF YOU STOPPED BEGGING FOR APPROVAL?

Not everything.

Your bills would still exist.

Your responsibilities wouldn't disappear.

The consequences of poor decisions wouldn't magically vanish.

And people would still have opinions about you.

But perhaps a few things would begin changing.

You might finally have an honest conversation you've been postponing.

You might admit that a career you pursued for approval no longer reflects your interests.

You might stop exaggerating your achievements to impress people who barely know you.

You might refuse a responsibility you genuinely cannot manage instead of saying yes and quietly resenting everybody involved.

You might publish an unpopular opinion while remaining willing to defend or revise it.

You might stop explaining every personal decision to people who were never entitled to control it.

And perhaps, for the first time in a long while, you'd discover the difference between being respected and being constantly approved of.

Because those things aren't identical.

**Some people may respect you more when they discover that your principles don't disappear whenever they disagree with you.**

Others may leave.

And that possibility is exactly why courage is required.

## X. BEYOND THE INSPIRATIONAL MYTH: INTEGRITY WITHOUT THE PROMISE OF APPLAUSE

People often conclude that the courage to be disliked is really the courage to be loved for who you truly are.

That's beautiful.

But I think it makes a promise life doesn't always keep.

Because what if you become more honest and some people genuinely stop loving you?

What if you set a reasonable boundary and a relationship ends?

What if you stop performing the role everyone expects, and the applause never returns?

What then?

Do you abandon your convictions because authenticity failed to produce a more appreciative audience?

**If your freedom still depends on eventually being loved for your authenticity, haven't you simply replaced one form of approval-seeking with another?**

Perhaps the real courage is more uncomfortable than that.

Perhaps it's being willing to act with integrity even when no one celebrates you.

To be misunderstood without immediately manufacturing a more acceptable version of yourself.

To make difficult decisions without demanding that everybody agree.

To remain kind even when people dislike you.

And to remain accountable even when you believe you're being unfairly judged.

Not because rejection is wonderful.

It isn't.

But because your life cannot become an endless negotiation with everybody else's expectations.

## XI. WINNING A COMPETITION WITHOUT A FINISH LINE: THE FINAL QUESTION

Think about the people you're trying to impress.

Your parents.

Your friends.

Your colleagues.

Your relatives.

Your followers.

The people who doubted you.

The people who laughed at your dreams.

The people you secretly hope will one day regret underestimating you.

How much of your life is still being organised around their imagined reactions?

How many achievements are you pursuing partly because you want someone to notice?

How much success would still matter to you if the people you wanted to impress never discovered it?

That's a question I find particularly disturbing.

**If nobody ever applauded your success, would you still consider it successful?**

If nobody congratulated you for becoming wealthy, would the money still matter?

If nobody admired your career, would you still enjoy the work?

If nobody praised your generosity, would you still give?

If nobody validated your convictions, would you still have the courage to examine and defend them?

Because perhaps the greatest tragedy isn't being disliked.

Perhaps it's reaching the end of your life and discovering that you spent most of it playing a character designed by other people's expectations.

You were the perfect child.

The respectable professional.

The agreeable friend.

The impressive neighbour.

The person who never disappointed anybody.

Everybody was happy with you.

**Except perhaps the one person who had to live your entire life from beginning to end.**

You.

And that's where I think *The Courage to Be Disliked* becomes more than a self-help book.

It becomes an interrogation.

Not an invitation to become selfish.

Not permission to become insensitive.

But a challenge to stop confusing acceptance with identity.

I don't believe we should live as though other people's opinions mean nothing.

Sometimes they reveal truths we desperately need to hear.

But I also don't believe we should hand over the responsibility for our lives to an audience that will never agree unanimously about how we should live.

So here's the question I'm leaving in my Crazy Journal:

**HOW MUCH OF THE PERSON YOU ARE TODAY IS ACTUALLY YOU, AND HOW MUCH IS A CHARACTER YOU CREATED TO AVOID BEING DISLIKED?**

And here's an even harder one.

**If being completely honest about who you are meant losing the approval of people you've spent years trying to impress, would you still choose honesty?**

Don't answer too quickly.

Because it's easy to celebrate authenticity when authenticity is popular.

It's easy to claim independence when everybody supports your decisions.

It's easy to say you don't care what people think when people are thinking wonderful things about you.

The real test comes when your convictions cost you something.

And maybe that's what courage actually looks like.

Not being loud.

Not being rude.

Not deliberately becoming controversial.

Not declaring war on everybody who disagrees with you.

But having the strength to live responsibly without requiring universal approval.

**You don't need everybody to like you.**

You need enough honesty to recognise who you are, enough humility to keep improving, and enough courage to accept that some people may never approve.

Because the moment your life requires everybody's permission...

**It is no longer entirely your life.**
